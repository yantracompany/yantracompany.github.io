Curious Findings in Recent GenAI Research

Generative AI research is moving quickly, and along the way, researchers have uncovered some surprising and sometimes counter-intuitive patterns. From creativity studies to environmental concerns, here are some of the most curious findings shaping the conversation today.

Humans + GenAI vs. GenAI Alone

A meta-analysis of over 8,200 participants found that GenAI alone performs similarly to humans on creative tasks, but when humans collaborate with GenAI, the results are significantly better. The catch? Diversity of ideas tends to drop when humans and AI generate together, leading to more convergent, "same-y" outputs.

Personas to Boost Idea Diversity

To address this narrowing effect, some researchers experimented with multiple AI personas—models simulating different backgrounds or preferences. When humans worked with these varied prompts, the resulting outputs regained or even surpassed the diversity of purely human ideation. In other words, diversity in prompts → diversity in outcomes.

Shifting Student Attitudes

In just one year, the percentage of computing students who reported never using ChatGPT fell from ~34% to only ~6%. GenAI is rapidly becoming normalized in education. Interestingly, frequent daily use actually dipped a bit, hinting that students may now be using AI more strategically rather than habitually.

Trust Varies by Task

Large-scale surveys show researchers are far more comfortable using GenAI for language editing or data analysis than for experiment design or peer review. Junior researchers are often more open to broader uses, while senior academics and non-technical fields remain cautious.

Ethics and Transparency Challenges

Across fields, concerns about transparency, reproducibility, bias, and hallucinations remain top of mind. Many papers argue that disclosure of how and when GenAI was used should become standard practice, but one-size-fits-all policies may not work—discipline-specific norms will be essential.

Environmental Footprint

Training, fine-tuning, and deploying large models comes with significant energy and water costs. Data centers powering GenAI consume increasing electricity, while cooling and hardware manufacturing also contribute to the environmental load. Researchers are calling for more efficient model architectures and transparent reporting of AI’s footprint.

“Generative AI doesn’t just change how we create—it changes what we value in creativity, collaboration, and sustainability.”

Overall, these findings remind us that GenAI is both a powerful collaborator and a disruptive force. It can amplify creativity but also homogenize it; democratize access but also raise ethical and environmental questions. Staying curious about these nuances will help us shape AI into a tool that serves society more thoughtfully.
